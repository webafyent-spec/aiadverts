Landing a retail listing is a milestone every small food brand works towards for years. The sauce that started at a weekend market, the biltong recipe perfected at home, the rusks that friends kept asking for, finally on a shelf in a national chain. And then the second problem arrives: the product is now one of forty in its section, and shoppers walk past it at walking pace.

A retail listing puts you in the store. It doesn't make anyone pick you up. That job falls to the marketing around the launch, and for most small food brands the marketing budget was spent getting listed in the first place.

## Why a Pack Shot Isn't Enough

Most food brands arrive at launch with one set of photographs: the product on a white background, taken for the retailer's listing form. It's necessary, and it does almost nothing to make someone want the product.

What actually sells packaged food is appetite and occasion. The sauce on something sizzling. The biltong in a hand at a rugby braai. The rusk dunked in morning coffee. Producing that kind of imagery usually means:

- **A food stylist and photographer** for each hero dish or serving suggestion
- **Props and a set** that match the occasion you're selling into
- **Separate shoots** for each product in the range, and again for each new flavour
- **Promotional assets on retail deadlines**, since in-store promotions run on fixed calendars that don't wait for your shoot date

For a brand with three or four products, that bill arrives at exactly the moment cash is tightest.

## Launch Week Is Not the Finish Line

The brands that stick on retail shelves are rarely the ones with the best launch. They're the ones that keep showing up after it. A product that's still being talked about three months in, with fresh recipes, seasonal occasions and regular promotions, gives both shoppers and buyers a reason to keep it in the range.

That kind of consistency is where most small brands fall away, because every new piece of content has traditionally meant another shoot.
